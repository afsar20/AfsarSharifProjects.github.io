01 / Overview

What explains a change in trading results?

A trading strategy can lose money because the trades cost more to complete, because its price forecasts become less useful, or both. I built a simulated trading record to check whether these causes could be identified separately.

The simulation contains 100,000 orders. An order is an instruction to buy or sell; a fill is the part that actually trades. All orders, prices, fills and problems in this study were generated for the experiment.

100,000Orders in the simulation
5.00 bpsTotal shortfall against the initial trading decision
61Trading days reserved for the final test

A basis point (bp) is 0.01%. A cost of five basis points is about $500 for every $1 million of intended trading. Shortfall is the difference between the result at the original decision price and the actual outcome, including fees and the opportunity missed on unfilled orders.

Result. The checks picked up an execution-cost problem and a later decline in forecast quality as separate events. This shows that the calculations and alerts work in the designed example. It does not establish their accuracy on real trading records.

02 / Data and terms

What the records contain

The records cover 250 trading days in 2025, with three fictional venues and three strategy groups. The only real market input is a measure of recent market volatility, meaning how much market returns varied. Each day’s input uses earlier days only.

Some orders are only partly filled. Duplicate messages, an unmatched fill and an invalid price are deliberately included to check that they are handled before results are calculated.

Execution
Completing an instruction to buy or sell in the market.
Parent order / fill
The parent is the full trading instruction. It may complete through several smaller fills, or remain partly unfilled.
Venue
A place where a trade can be executed. Venues A, B and C here are fictional.
Trading signal
A forecast used to decide which trades to make. Signal decay means that forecast becomes less useful.
Spread
The difference between the best buying price and the best selling price.
Participation rate
The order’s share of market trading volume over its chosen period.
Latency
The time taken to process an instruction. The chart measures this delay in milliseconds.
Profit and loss (P&L)
The money gained or lost by the trades after they are marked at the final price; net P&L also subtracts fees.
Cost estimate / residual
The model estimates the cost expected for an order’s conditions. The residual is actual cost minus that estimate; a positive value means the trade cost more than expected.

03 / Method

How I separated the causes

  1. Check the records. Match each fill to its order, remove exact duplicate messages and reject invalid entries. Stop the calculation if the fills exceed the amount ordered.
  2. Explain the trading cost. Separate price movement before the order reaches the market, later market movement, the execution price, the missed opportunity on unfilled orders and fees.
  3. Estimate normal costs. Fit a model using the first 126 days. It accounts for differences such as spread, recent market movement and order size relative to trading volume. Use the next 63 days to choose the model settings and alert threshold.
  4. Test on later days. Apply those fixed settings to the final 61 days, starting 2025-10-06. Compare actual costs with the estimate and check forecast quality separately.
More detail on the model and accounting

The cost model uses Ridge regression: a linear prediction method that limits the size of its fitted coefficients to reduce overfitting. Overfitting means learning patterns specific to the training sample that fail on new data.

For each venue, daily average cost errors are compared with the middle value and spread of errors in the 63-day calibration sample. A large positive error triggers an alert. A separate alert checks unusually weak signed price moves relative to the original forecast.

The accounting check is: profit at the decision price − actual net profit = total shortfall. Every order reconciled to within $9.73e-10, a numerical rounding difference far below one cent.

The SQL tables and complete formulas are available in the methods file. The model never sees the labels identifying which dates contain a simulated problem.

05 / Results

What the experiment showed

Execution cost and delay rose together

Venue B’s cost increase runs from 2025-11-04 to 2025-11-24. During those days, its trades cost more than the model expected and its processing delay rises. The other venues do not show the same break.

Across all 61 test days, Venue B averaged 1.46 basis points above the estimate. This average includes the days before and after the problem; it is smaller than the six-basis-point increase deliberately inserted during the incident.

The forecast problem appeared in a different series

Strategy group A starts to miss its original price forecasts from 2025-12-10. Its forecast errors worsen separately from the earlier execution issue. Checking only the final profit would hide that distinction.

Unfilled orders have a cost too

If every intended share had traded at the initial decision price, valuing the positions at the final simulated prices would have produced a $13.55 million gain. The actual net result was a loss of $337, after execution costs, unfilled orders and fees. These are simulated amounts, used to demonstrate why the whole order needs to be measured.

Implication. Investigate the execution process when cost and processing delay rise together. Review the trading forecast when expected price moves fail to appear. The two problems may require different fixes.

The monitor found all one-basis-point increases, but only 10 of the 45 quarter-basis-point cases. Small changes are harder to distinguish from ordinary variation. In the zero-increase control, it raised no alerts.

How the uncertainty range was calculated

I repeatedly sampled five-day sections of the final test period and recalculated each venue’s average cost error. Keeping adjacent days together preserves some of the dependence between trading days. This is called a block bootstrap.

The central 95% of these resampled averages gives an uncertainty range. It describes how sensitive this sample average is to the days included; it is not a guarantee about future costs.

07 / Limits

What needs testing on real trades

The simulation uses a simple cost model and deliberately inserted problems. Real trading includes changing order flow, market conditions, routing decisions and missing information. Similar-looking alerts could have different causes.

The comparison between venues is descriptive. It cannot show that sending the same real order to another venue would have improved its execution. That would require a fair comparison of comparable orders, or a controlled routing experiment.

A practical next step would use real orders, quotes and timestamps, then test how often alerts lead to a useful investigation. The current website shows a saved experiment, not a live trading monitor.
